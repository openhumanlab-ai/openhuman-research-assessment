**1. Choi et al. (2026), "What Makes a Sale?" — purchase choices**
[arxiv.org/abs/2604.04468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04468) (KAIST and GS Retail; accepted at COLM 2026)
- **Varied:** a buyer persona with gender and three binary traits, including price consciousness (sensitive vs indifferent), plus product price at five levels. Eight LLMs played buyer and seller in a simulated retail conversation.
- **Measured:** purchase rate and price elasticity by trait; human annotators judged whether buyer transcripts matched the assigned trait.
- **Found:** elasticity was 2.58 for price-sensitive buyers and 0.76 for price-indifferent ones (p<0.001). Annotators recognised the assigned price trait 93–99.7% of the time.
- **Limits:** the trait effect is not compared with real purchase data. The trait is binary and arrives bundled with other traits in a multi-turn sales dialogue, so it is not an isolated choice. I could not read the limitations section.

**2. Allouah et al. (2025), "What Is Your AI Agent Buying?" — purchase choices**
[arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02630) (Columbia, Yale, MyCustomAI; preprint)
- **Varied:** position, price, rating, reviews and badges for eight products on a mock shopping page, across six frontier models. As a robustness check, the prompt said the user is price sensitive.
- **Measured:** product chosen, analysed with a conditional logit model.
- **Found:** the price-sensitive line "steepens the agent's price sensitivity as intended", but "significant position effects remain". The price coefficient roughly tripled or more (for example −1.76 to −5.64 for Claude Sonnet 4; table values not read verbatim).
- **Limits:** the persona line is a side check, tested together with an "ignore position" instruction. The model shops for a user and does not simulate a person. There is no like-for-like human benchmark.

**3. Gui & Toubia (2023, revised 2025), "The Challenge of Using LLMs to Simulate Human Behavior" — purchase choices**
[arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524)
- **Varied:** price of 40 grocery products at 11 levels, with the prompt "You, AI, are a customer" (GPT-4o-mini, 50 draws each). Later they added persona covariates and revealed the experimental design to the model.
- **Measured:** purchase probability against 991 Prolific respondents, and what the model assumed about unstated context.
- **Found:** changing the price also changed the model's assumed competitor price and past price paid. Human demand sloped down; the model's was an inverted U. Adding persona covariates helped up to a point and then hurt. A tightwad–spendthrift score gave the largest reported gain (error 0.23 to 0.10; not read verbatim).
- **Limits:** price is manipulated, not the trait. The main experiment uses one model and a yes/no purchase of a single product.

**4. Brand, Israeli & Ngwe (2023), "Using LLMs for Market Research" — purchase choices**
[papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4395751](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4395751) (blocked to me; details come from the [HBS Working Knowledge article](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/can-ai-predict-whether-shoppers-would-pick-crest-over-colgate) and the [MSI abstract](https://www.msi.org/working-paper/using-llms-for-market-research-2/))
- **Varied:** prices, product attributes and stated annual income ($50,000–$120,000) in prompts asking GPT-3/3.5 whether a customer would buy.
- **Measured:** choice shares, demand curves and willingness to pay from conjoint-style tasks.
- **Found:** higher stated income went with higher price tolerance. Willingness to pay for fluoride toothpaste was $3.40 against $3.27 in a human study. The later abstract says estimates are often inaccurate and sometimes wrong-signed (from a search snippet).
- **Limits:** old models and few product categories. Income is a demographic proxy, not a price-sensitivity trait. Read the primary paper before citing it.

**5. Horton, Filippas & Manning (2023, revised 2026), "Homo Silicus" — not purchase choices**
[arxiv.org/abs/2301.07543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07543)
- **Varied:** one-sentence endowments such as "You only care about fairness between players" and "You are a socialist" through "libertarian", plus the size of a snow-shovel price hike ($16–$100).
- **Measured:** dictator-game allocations and fairness ratings of the price hike.
- **Found:** choices moved in the endowed direction, and larger hikes were rated less fair. With no endowment, models differed from each other and from the original human participants.
- **Limits:** the original studies are probably in the training data. Human comparisons come only from the original papers, and the authors say results need empirical confirmation.

**Also seen, not ranked**
- [Kim et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09977): an "on a tight budget" prompt moved price elasticity from about 0 to between −2.3 and −5.6. It is a three-week-old preprint with no human study of its own, and it manipulates budget, not price weight.
- [Maier et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08338), PyMC Labs and Colgate-Palmolive: demographic personas against about 9,300 human responses. Low-income personas reported lower purchase intent, as humans did; price was not manipulated.
- [Mohammadi (2024, revised 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01332): a single-author preprint. Adding "You are a consumer" moved one model's probability of choosing the cheaper flight from 0.915 to about 0.5; five blank lines alone moved it to 0.593.
- [Choi et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03262): personas built from 522 real people predicted individual pay-what-you-want choices under 5% of the time, though group-level patterns were reasonable.

Nothing from Anthropic or other labs surfaced, though I did not search for that specifically.

**What this suggests for the research question**
- **Direction is close to given.** Every study that adds a price or budget cue finds a shift in the expected direction, so "does the trait sentence move choices" is nearly a manipulation check.
- **Nuisance effects rival trait effects.** Position and formatting persist alongside the persona (papers 2 and Mohammadi), so the trait effect is best judged against the option-order effect.
- **Trait wording can change assumed context.** Paper 3 shows the model infers unstated background; the supplied personas already hold affordability constant, which helps separate price weight from budget.
- **No one validates the size of the shift.** None of these compares a trait-induced shift with humans who have that trait measured, and we have no human data either, so the pilot can only test internal validity.

**What the paper is**
Choi et al. present RetailSim, a simulator in which one LLM plays a seller and another plays a buyer through a whole sale. Its purpose is to test retail strategies, and the persona work is the validation step, not the main aim. Link: [arxiv.org/abs/2604.04468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04468) (KAIST and GS Retail, COLM 2026).

**Method**
- **Pipeline:** each run covers one product and five stages: seller pitch, pre-purchase questions from the buyer, purchase decision, post-purchase support, and reviews.
- **Decision:** the buyer returns JSON with `will_purchase` true or false and a quantity. It is buy or not buy on one product, not a choice between options.
- **Products and price:** products are sampled from the Amazon Reviews '23 dataset. The prompt shows an original price, a discount rate and a discounted price.
- **Buyer persona:** gender plus three binary traits (pickiness, price consciousness, rationality), giving 16 combinations. The price wording is:
  - price-sensitive: "You care deeply about price, value-for-money, discounts, and hidden costs. You compare prices, ask about deals, and evaluate whether the cost is justified."
  - price-indifferent: "You don't focus on price. If you want it, cost is secondary. You rarely mention or ask about pricing."
  - The block ends with an instruction that behaviour "MUST strictly reflect ALL THREE personality traits".
- **Models:** eight LLMs, each at its default temperature, pooled in the analysis.
- **Elasticity:** for each product and each trait group, they regress log purchase rate on log price ratio. The two groups are compared with a one-tailed paired t-test across products.

**Results**

| Test | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Persona recognisability | 2,304 output pairs from opposite trait levels; three MTurk annotators pick which shows the target trait | Buyer price consciousness identified 93.4–99.7% of the time across models (agreement α 0.77) |
| Price and demand | 8 products at five prices, −10% to +10% | Purchase rate falls from 64.8% to 56.6% |
| Elasticity by trait | Same runs, split by price trait | 2.58 for price-sensitive vs 0.76 for price-indifferent, p<0.001 |
| Purchase rate by trait | Appendix G; run set not stated | 48.9% for price-sensitive vs 67.7% for price-indifferent |
| Gender | 1,920 runs on fashion products | Female buyers buy women's products far more (55.8% vs 32.5%); the gap disappears when the persona is removed |
| Ablation | 9,600 runs with the persona block removed, then price framing also removed | Elasticity ratio between the two trait groups: 3.40× originally, 3.74× with no persona, 2.25× with no persona and no price anchor |

In a separate section with no personas, most models behaved as price-indifferent buyers by default; only Gemini-3.1-Pro came out price-sensitive.

**The authors' conclusion**
- They say RetailSim "successfully reproduces key patterns such as demographic purchasing behavior, the price–demand relationship, and heterogeneous price elasticity".
- They read the high annotator accuracy as showing that persona traits are reliably preserved across models.
- From the ablation they conclude the gender pattern comes from the persona injection, while the elasticity gap reflects "a baseline price reasoning intrinsic to the models".

**What to be careful about (my reading)**
- **The ablation undercuts the headline.** With the persona removed, the paper says the buyer "cannot know its own trait labels", yet the sensitive and indifferent groups still differ by 3.74×, more than with the persona. The paper does not explain how groups that never see their label can differ, so the 2.58 vs 0.76 gap cannot be cleanly credited to the trait text.
- **The magnitudes do not add up.** The group elasticities average about 1.67, but the paper's pooled elasticity is 0.81 (Table 4), and Table 3 implies about 0.67 by my arithmetic. I would rely on the direction, not the size.
- **The trait wording prescribes behaviour.** The prompt tells the buyer to compare prices and ask about deals and to "strictly reflect" the trait, so the result is close to a compliance check.
- **Fidelity means recognisability.** Annotators judged whether the trait was visible in the text, not whether decisions resemble those of real price-sensitive people.
- **No human purchase data for trait effects.** They are compared with the literature by direction only; the one real-data check is review ratings.
- **Reporting gaps.** Runs per cell, the test statistic for the main elasticity result, and per-model breakdowns are not given, and there is no limitations section.

**Why it matters for our pilot**
- Their trait is delivered as a bundle of four attributes inside a sales conversation; ours is a single sentence on an isolated two-option choice, which is a cleaner test.
- Their no-persona ablation is the comparison to copy: our no-trait persona and the null persona play that role.
- Default model behaviour may not be neutral on price, so the baseline needs measuring, not assuming.

**In one sentence:** when you ask an LLM to play a customer and change the price, it does not only react to the price; it also changes its picture of the whole situation, which makes its answers unreliable as a stand-in for real people.

**Paper:** Gui & Toubia (2023, revised 2025), [arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15524)

**What they tested**
1. They told GPT-4o-mini "You, AI, are a customer" and asked whether it would buy a grocery product at a given price. They did this for 40 products, each at 11 prices from free up to double the normal price.
2. They asked 991 real people the same questions in an online survey, as a benchmark.
3. They asked the model side questions, such as what it paid last time, to see what it was assuming.
4. They tried two ways to improve the model's answers:
   - describing the customer in more detail (demographics first, then psychological traits);
   - telling the model outright that this is an experiment where the price is set at random.

**What they found**
- **The model's assumptions moved with the price.** Shown a higher price, it also assumed the customer had paid more last time, that competing products cost more, and that the product had more days until expiry. In a real experiment only the price changes.
- **Its buying pattern was wrong.** Real people bought less as the price rose. The model bought most at mid-range prices and less at both very low and very high prices.
- **More customer detail helped only up to a point.** With demographics alone, the model's predicted purchase rates were off by 0.225 on average, on a 0 to 1 scale. Adding one trait, a score for how reluctant the person is to spend money, cut the error to 0.097. Adding further traits pushed it back up to about 0.20.
- **The model overreacts to whatever the prompt mentions.** When the prompt stated a competitor's price, simulated customers bought whenever the product was no dearer and never otherwise, which is far more rigid than real shoppers.
- **Telling the model about the experiment helped.** In a separate test it reduced the error on every model tried; for GPT-4o-mini it fell from 0.532 to 0.397.

**What they concluded**
A simulated person exists only through the prompt. Anything the prompt leaves unsaid, the model fills in, and it fills it in differently in each condition. Adding detail closes those gaps but makes the model overreact to the details. Their recommended fix is to tell the model exactly what is being varied and how. More broadly, methods designed for human participants should not be assumed to carry over to LLMs.

**Pitfalls**
- **It is about price, not persona traits.** The spending-trait result is a by-product. Traits were added one after another in a fixed order, so we cannot tell how much each one matters in isolation.
- **Their fix changed two things at once.** The new prompt explained the experiment and also switched the model's role from being the customer to being an expert predicting the customer. It is unclear which change produced the improvement.
- **The human benchmark is stated intention.** People said what they would buy in a survey; nobody actually bought anything.
- **Humans and the model were asked differently.** Each person answered all 40 products in one sitting, while the model got a fresh, separate call for each. The authors acknowledge this.
- **Narrow model coverage.** The main experiment uses one model, and all models tested are from OpenAI.
- **The error stays large after the fix.** An error of 0.397 means predicted purchase rates were still off by about 40 percentage points on average.
- **Publication status.** It is a working paper with no journal listed.

> what is meant by Unintended Confounding in Blind LLM Simulations in chapter 2 of Gui & Toubia

**Claude**

_Tool actions:_

- `Bash`: Print the pages of section 2 not yet read — `S=<scratchpad> && cd "$S" && python3 -I pdfpages.py "$S/gui_dl/paper.pdf" 4,5,8,9 | cut -c1-700`

It means that when you change one thing in a prompt, the LLM silently changes other things too, so you can no longer tell what caused the difference in its answers.

**The three terms**
- **Blind:** the model is not told it is in an experiment. Each prompt shows only one condition (for example one price), with no mention that other prompts show other prices. This copies standard practice with human participants.
- **Confounding:** something other than the thing you varied also differs between conditions and could explain the result.
- **Unintended:** the researcher did not put that difference there. The model introduced it by filling in details the prompt left out.

**Why it happens with LLMs but not with people**
A real person arrives with a fixed history and situation. If you randomly show them a high or low price, their past purchases and the shop they are in stay the same, so only the price differs.

A simulated customer has no fixed background. The model invents it from the prompt each time. In real-world data, a high price usually goes with an upmarket shop, dearer competitors and a higher price last time. So when the model sees a high price, it assumes that whole situation.

**How they showed it**
- **Step 1:** they gave the model a price and asked it to fill in what the customer paid last time, the competitor's price and the days to expiry. All three rose with the price shown, although none should depend on it.
- **Step 2:** they asked whether it would buy. Because a higher price also implied a pricier setting, purchases did not fall steadily as price rose the way human purchases did.

The authors call this unsurprising in hindsight: the model is reproducing real correlations, which is useful for describing the world but wrong for a controlled experiment.

**Why it matters for our pilot**
Our design is exactly this kind of blind, one-condition-per-prompt setup. A sentence saying Alex gives high weight to price could also make the model assume Alex has less money or different tastes. The supplied personas guard against part of this by stating that all options are within budget.

**In one sentence:** an LLM asked to answer as a customer gives realistic answers for the average shopper, but when you tell it what kind of customer it is, it exaggerates the differences between groups far beyond what real people show.

**Paper:** Brand, Israeli & Ngwe, "Using LLMs for Market Research" (first circulated 2023; I read the October 2025 version). [MSI working paper page](https://www.msi.org/working-paper/using-llms-for-market-research-2/); the [SSRN page](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4395751) blocks automated access.

**What they tested**
1. They told GPT it was a randomly selected customer choosing between two products (toothpaste, laptops, tablets) or buying nothing. Brand, price and features were varied across prompts, and each prompt was repeated many times.
2. From those choices they estimated how much extra a customer would pay for each feature, and compared this with a published human study and five human surveys of their own.
3. They added customer details to the prompt (income, gender, race, political affiliation) and compared each simulated group with the matching group of real respondents.
4. They retrained GPT on earlier human survey answers (fine-tuning) to see if that brought it closer to people.

**What they found**
- **Averages were often realistic.** In the pilot, GPT's customers would pay $3.40 extra for fluoride in toothpaste, against $3.27 in a published human study.
- **But not always.** In their own later toothpaste study, GPT-3.5 valued fluoride at about three times the human figure ($8.20 against $2.60).
- **Customer details caused exaggeration.** For incomes under $50,000 versus over $150,000:
  - real people chose not to buy a laptop 49% and 47% of the time, while GPT's customers did so 95% and 34% of the time;
  - real people valued extra memory at $17.80 and $19.40, while GPT gave −$4.40 and $99.60.
- **The high-income customer went wrong.** GPT's high-income customer preferred higher prices, which no real group did.
- **Ordering sometimes right, sizes wrong.** GPT sometimes ranked groups correctly (for example, liking for a MacBook rising with income), but it applied the same ranking to every feature, unlike real people.
- **Retraining helped averages only.** It improved average answers, including for new features in the same product category. It did not help for new categories or for differences between groups.
- **Order and wording mattered.** GPT was significantly more likely to pick whichever option was listed first, so they showed each order half the time. Small wording changes also altered how often it declined to buy.

**What they concluded**
LLMs are a fast, cheap supplement for learning about the average customer and for narrowing down ideas before testing with people. They are not a replacement for human studies. The authors say they could not get GPT to meaningfully reflect different preferences across demographic groups, and that each customer segment may need its own retrained model.

**Pitfalls**
- **Old and narrow model coverage.** Most results use GPT-3.5 Turbo, and the group comparison rests on one model and one laptop study.
- **Demographics, not traits.** The customer details are income, gender and similar. Nobody was described as price-sensitive, so the evidence for our question is indirect.
- **The human benchmark is hypothetical.** People answered survey questions and bought nothing. The sample was younger, more educated and lower-income than the US population.
- **No uncertainty on the LLM figures.** The authors do not report margins of error for GPT's estimates, because it is unclear how to compute them.
- **Sensitive to prompt wording.** The authors list this themselves and did not try to optimise prompts.
- **Versions differ.** The 2023 version was more upbeat about income effects, according to a Harvard news article about it; I have not read that version. The paper is still a working paper, not a journal article.

Two rounds is the right call, and more would probably be overkill, with one exception below.

**Why two and not one:** a single round cannot tell a real pattern from chance. The second round is what shows how often the same prompt gives a different answer.

**Why not more:**

- **The price steps limit precision, not the number of answers.** The switch point can only be located to within one step of the ladder, and extra rounds do not make the steps finer.
- **Extra rounds only measure one kind of uncertainty.** They repeat the identical prompt, so they tighten the estimate for that exact wording. The bigger unknowns are wording, scenario and model, and no number of rounds touches those. If you have spare budget, a third wording or finer steps near the switch point would tell you more.
- **The brief asks for a small pilot.** 840 calls is already generous, each extra round costs about 18 minutes of your four hours, and the scoring rewards reasoning over call volume.

**The exception:** if rounds 1 and 2 disagree often, your reading rule becomes fragile. "Highest price still paid" takes the highest step where 2 of 4 answers pay, so one noisy step high up the ladder can set it. As an illustration, if answers above the true switch point were 10% random, there is roughly a one-in-three chance that one of eight higher steps shows a spurious 2 of 4. With four rounds that falls to about 4%. I do not know the model's noise level yet; experiment 0 had one call per cell.

So I would run two, then look at how often the rounds agree. import json


def get_answer(persona: str, scenario: str, extra_percent: float, order: str, round_number: int) -> tuple[str, str]:
    """Return (chosen option, explanation), e.g. get_answer("profile-02", "dinner", 75, "reversed", 1)."""
    condition = f"{persona};{scenario};pct{round(extra_percent * 10):04d};{order}"
    for line in open(f"../runs/rq1-round{round_number}/results.jsonl", encoding="utf-8"):
        result = json.loads(line)

- **Returned:** the chosen option and an explanation.

Every combination was asked once: 5 × 3 × 28 × 2 × 2 = 1,680 calls. They were run in two batches of 840: the original 14 prices first, then the 14 extra prices added after seeing the first results.

**How the answers are counted**

Each answer is reduced to one fact: did it pick the more expensive option or not. Nothing is averaged in two steps; the answers that belong together go into one pile, and the chart shows the share of that pile that picked the expensive option.
